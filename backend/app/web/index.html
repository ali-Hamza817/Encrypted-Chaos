How it works

One pipeline, two readouts

The same plaintext–ciphertext pairs feed both the defensive profile and the offensive model. They are meant to be read together: a scheme can score almost perfectly on the classical metrics and still lose ground to the learned attack.

Research questions

What the thesis answers

Reproducing the 2019 attack is not a contribution in 2026. These push the work toward generalization — and toward whether the classical metrics predict resistance to data-driven attacks at all.

  1. Under a fixed key, can a CNN / U-Net reconstruct unseen plaintext images, and at what fidelity?

    same-key known-plaintext attack · baseline

  2. Does a model trained on many keys generalize to ciphertext from an unseen key?

    ★ primary contribution · cross-key generalization

  3. Does a model trained on one chaotic map transfer to a different chaotic construction?

    ★ secondary · cross-algorithm transfer

  4. Under identical budgets, are chaos schemes more susceptible than AES-CTR on the same images?

    control · fairness bound

  5. Which components drive attack success — permutation vs diffusion, rounds, keying mode?

    ★ vulnerability attribution · ablations

  6. How many known pairs are needed to reach a target reconstruction quality?

    data-efficiency curve

Reading the numbers honestly. A high score means the model recovered perceptual structure — not that the secret key was extracted. A static keystream reduces to a fixed position-dependent pad any learner can memorise; that configuration is the known-vulnerable baseline, shown for contrast. The hardened-chaos and AES results are the meaningful comparison.
